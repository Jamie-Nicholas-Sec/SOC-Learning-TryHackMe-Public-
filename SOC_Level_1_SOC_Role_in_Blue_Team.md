## Overview

This module helped me develop a clearer understanding of **where a Security Operations Centre (SOC) fits within an organisation**, how different security professionals work together, and how potential incidents move between teams.

As a Cyber Security student working in IT support, I found it particularly useful to compare the SOC structure with the roles, escalation paths and responsibilities I already encounter in a service desk environment.

## Blue Team and the wider security function

The **Blue Team** focuses on defending systems, detecting threats, responding to incidents and improving security controls. This differs from the **Red Team**, which simulates attacker behaviour to identify weaknesses under an authorised scope.

I also learned how defensive teams interact with other areas of security, including governance, risk and compliance (GRC), threat intelligence, engineering and incident response. Each has a different responsibility, but they contribute to the same objective: reducing organisational risk.

## Roles I explored

| Role | Main responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| SOC Level 1 Analyst | Monitor and triage alerts, gather initial evidence, document findings and escalate when necessary. |
| SOC Level 2 Analyst | Perform deeper investigations and support more complex incident analysis. |
| SOC Engineer | Maintain and improve monitoring infrastructure, detection tooling and integrations. |
| SOC Manager / Team Lead | Coordinate SOC operations, people, priorities and escalation processes. |
| Cyber Incident Response Team (CIRT) | Coordinate investigation, containment, recovery and communication during significant incidents. |
| Threat Intelligence Analyst | Research threats and produce intelligence that informs defensive decisions. |
| GRC Specialist | Help manage security policies, risk, compliance and assurance activities. |

These responsibilities can overlap or be organised differently depending on the employer. A small organisation may combine several roles, while a larger one may have more specialist teams.

## Internal SOC versus managed security services

I learned that organisations can operate an internal SOC or use a **Managed Security Service Provider (MSSP)** for some or all monitoring and response services.

An internal SOC develops familiarity with its own organisation's systems, people and risks. An MSSP may monitor several customers with different environments and service agreements. Both models require effective triage, documentation, communication and escalation.

One important reflection was that **neither model is automatically quieter or more demanding**. Workload depends on the organisation, its threat exposure, staffing, tooling and incidents at the time.

## My reflections

The role structure felt familiar because of my experience as a Service Desk Analyst. In IT support, I regularly work with incidents, troubleshooting, clear ticket documentation and escalation to more experienced colleagues or specialist teams.

SOC work applies many of the same professional habits to security-specific problems. The technical focus changes, but the need to assess priority, understand the limits of my responsibility and involve the right people remains essential.

I particularly enjoyed learning **why different security roles handle different kinds of work**. Understanding the people and processes behind the tools is just as important as knowing how to investigate an alert.

## Key takeaways

- A SOC is a team-based operation, not just a collection of monitoring tools.
- Level 1 analysts play an important role in initial triage and escalation.
- Security engineers, incident responders, GRC teams and threat researchers contribute different expertise.
- Clear ownership and escalation paths support effective incident handling.
- My existing IT support experience provides transferable skills for a future junior SOC role.
